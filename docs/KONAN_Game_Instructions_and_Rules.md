**SECTION 1  Overview & Core Objective**

KONAN (Knowledge Over Noise And Narratives) is a cooperative, real-time Media and Information Literacy simulation for two players. Set inside a simulated social media ecosystem, KONAN places one player inside a flooded information feed and another inside a verification dashboard — and challenges them to stop the spread of misinformation together, under pressure, before the clock runs out.

KONAN is not a quiz. It is not a passive awareness tool. It is an active, skill-building environment designed around a single pedagogical principle:

| *You cannot learn to navigate noise by reading about it. You learn by navigating it — under pressure, together.* |
| :---- |

**What Players Will Learn**

* How to identify the specific signals that distinguish verified content from misinformation and misleading narratives.

* How to communicate observations precisely under time and social pressure.

* How to run a full verification process — not just spot surface-level artifacts.

* How to apply deliberate, process-driven thinking before sharing any piece of content online.

* How to teach these skills to others — transitioning from player to MIL advocate.

**Key Design Principles**

| Principle | What It Means in KONAN |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Deliberation Over Speed** | The game rewards thoroughness. A slow, correct verification earns more than a fast, lucky one. |
| **Cooperation Over Competition** | Neither player can win alone. The Scroller cannot verify; the Verifier cannot see. Both skills are required. |
| **Process Over Artifacts** | Players learn to run a verification process — not just look for visual tells that AI will soon make undetectable. |
| **Nuance Over Binary** | Content is Verified, Misleading, or Fabricated — reflecting how real misinformation actually exists in the world. |
| **Reflection Over Score** | Every round ends with a structured debrief, not just a leaderboard. The debrief is where the deepest learning happens. |

**SECTION 2  Players, Roles & The Cooperative Split**

KONAN requires two players per session. Each player occupies one of two asymmetric roles. These roles are deliberately incomplete — each player can do what the other cannot, making cooperation the only path to success.

| PLAYER A  —  THE SCROLLER | PLAYER B  —  THE VERIFIER |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Device:** Mobile phone or tablet **Interface:** Simulated social media feed | **Device:** Desktop or laptop computer **Interface:** Verification dashboard |
| **What the Scroller CAN do** See the full social media post: image, caption, account name, follower count, and verified badge status. Describe what they see verbally (or via text relay in Accessibility Mode) to the Verifier. Return to a post if instructed by the Verifier (with a time penalty). Execute the final verdict: swipe Share (Verified/Misleading) or Report (Fabricated). | **What the Verifier CAN do** Access the full verification dashboard: domain analysis tool, WHOIS lookup, account history checker, image metadata reader, and cross-source corroboration tool. Run keyword searches and reverse queries on any term the Scroller describes. Direct the Scroller's attention: ask specific questions about what to look at. Recommend the verdict; the Scroller executes it. |
| **What the Scroller CANNOT do** Access any verification tools during active play. Search the post content on their own device. Pass the device to the Verifier mid-scenario. | **What the Verifier CANNOT do** See the social media feed at any point during active play. Make the final swipe action themselves. Assume any detail the Scroller has not described. |

| The Fundamental Rule of Role Separation The Verifier's entire decision-making chain depends on the accuracy and completeness of what the Scroller describes. If the Scroller omits a key detail, the Verifier cannot catch it. This is not a flaw — it is the lesson: incomplete communication is how misinformation spreads through trusted networks. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 3  Game Structure — The Three-Round Session**

Every KONAN session is divided into three mandatory rounds. Role rotation is built into the structure and is not optional. Both players must complete both roles before the session ends. Round 3 removes the role split entirely, functioning as a solo transfer assessment.

| Round | Player A | Player B | Purpose |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Round 1** | Scroller | Verifier | Player A builds observational and communication skills. Player B builds tool-use and analytical skills. |
| **Round 2** | Verifier | Scroller | Roles reverse. Each player acquires the skill set they lacked. Empathy for the other role is built. |
| **Round 3** | **Solo — no role split** Both players share a single interface and complete all verification steps independently. |  | Assessment round. Tests whether players can apply both skill sets simultaneously — the real-world MIL context. |

Each round consists of 5 scenarios. A full session therefore covers 15 scenarios total across all three rounds. Scenario selection is different for each round — players do not see the same post twice within a session.

| Why Three Rounds Are Mandatory A player who completes only Round 1 leaves knowing how to describe a post but not how to verify it. That is the opposite of the game's stated goal. The three-round structure ensures every player exits with both the observational skills of the Scroller and the analytical skills of the Verifier — and has practiced applying them together in Round 3\. |
| :---- |

**Per-Round Timing**

| Phase | Duration | Rounds 1 & 2 | Round 3 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Scenarios | 5 per round | 5 min per scenario | 7 min per scenario |
| Instant Reveal | After each scenario | 2 min | 3 min |
| Round Debrief | End of round | 10 min | 15 min |
| **Total per Round** | **\~45 min (Rounds 1–2)  /  \~60 min (Round 3\)** |  |  |

**SECTION 4  The Verify-First Mechanic — How a Scenario Works**

Each scenario follows a strict six-phase sequence. No phase may be skipped. The structure is the same for all rounds; only the available time and role configuration differ.

**Phase 1 — The Hold (10 Seconds, Mandatory)**

When a new post appears on the Scroller's feed, a 10-second mandatory hold timer activates automatically. The Scroller cannot swipe or act during this period. The Verifier cannot input queries yet.

The Hold exists for one reason: to replace the instinct to react with the habit of pause. It is the first intervention against reflexive sharing, delivered at the mechanical level rather than as advice.

| The Hold Is Non-Negotiable The Hold cannot be skipped at any difficulty level. It is not a timer — it is a designed pause that begins to rewire the habit of immediate reaction. Any game mode that removes the Hold is removing the primary MIL intervention. |
| :---- |

**Phase 2 — Initial Description (Scroller)**

When the Hold ends, the Scroller has 45 seconds to deliver an Initial Description. The description must cover all five items in the Description Checklist before the Verifier may ask clarifying questions.

**The Description Checklist — All Five Must Be Covered**

| \# | Item | Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | **Account name and handle** | "Account name is AgriNewsNow, handle @AgriNewsNow." |
| 2 | **Account age and verification status** | "Account was created 2 days ago. Not verified." |
| 3 | **Post type** | "It is a photo post — no video, no link." |
| 4 | **Claim being made** | "Caption claims a typhoon destroyed 80% of rice crops in Central Luzon this morning." |
| 5 | **Notable visual or textual anomalies** | "The lighting in the photo looks overly cinematic — shadows seem inconsistent." |

If the Scroller fails to cover all five items within 45 seconds, the Verifier may prompt: "Checklist incomplete — what is the account age?" The Scroller must return to the post to answer, triggering a 30-second time penalty.

**Phase 3 — Verification Dialogue**

Once the Initial Description is complete, the active Verification Dialogue begins. The Verifier runs the verification dashboard tools and asks targeted questions based on what the Scroller has described.

**Verification Tools Available to the Verifier**

* Account History Checker — shows account creation date, follower growth rate, posting frequency, and historical username changes.

* Domain Authority Tool — rates the credibility of any URL or publication cited in the post based on editorial standards, fact-checking history, and domain age.

* Image Metadata Reader — extracts file creation date, GPS coordinates (if embedded), device model, and editing software fingerprints from uploaded images.

* WHOIS Lookup — reveals domain registration details for any website linked or referenced.

* Cross-Source Corroboration Tool — searches a curated database of verified news sources for corroborating or contradicting coverage of the claimed event.

* Keyword & Phrase Search — flags whether specific phrases have appeared in known disinformation campaigns.

The Verifier may run as many tools as the time allows. There is no penalty for running more tools. There is a penalty for making a verdict recommendation without completing at least two tool checks.

| Rule: Minimum Two Tool Checks Required A verdict recommendation made without completing at least two independent tool checks is classified as an Unsupported Verdict and incurs a 25% score penalty on that scenario, regardless of whether the verdict was correct. This rule enforces the process — not just the outcome. |
| :---- |

**Phase 4 — Verdict Recommendation**

When the Verifier has completed their analysis, they make a Verdict Recommendation to the Scroller. The recommendation must include three components:

1. The verdict: Verified, Misleading, or Fabricated.

2. The primary signal: the single most decisive piece of evidence supporting the verdict.

3. The confidence level: a number from 1 (very uncertain) to 5 (certain).

Example of a complete Verdict Recommendation:

| *"I recommend Fabricated. Primary signal: the account was created 48 hours ago and has gained 12,000 followers in that time — a bot-typical growth pattern. No corroborating coverage exists in any verified agricultural or news source. Confidence: 4."* |
| :---- |

**Phase 5 — Scroller Decision**

The Scroller is not required to follow the Verifier's recommendation. They may override it if they have observed something on the feed that the Verifier has not accounted for. However, any override requires the Scroller to state their reason aloud before executing the swipe.

* If the Scroller follows the recommendation and is correct: full score for both players.

* If the Scroller follows the recommendation and is incorrect: half score for both players.

* If the Scroller overrides and is correct: full score for the Scroller only; the Verifier receives the score they would have earned on their recommendation.

* If the Scroller overrides and is incorrect: zero for both players on that scenario.

This rule creates genuine agency for the Scroller while making overrides a deliberate, accountable act — not a casual impulse.

**Phase 6 — Instant Reveal**

Immediately after every verdict — correct or incorrect — the Instant Reveal screen activates. This cannot be skipped. The Instant Reveal shows:

* The correct verdict and why.

* The full verification trail: what all six tools would have shown if queried.

* For incorrect verdicts: the specific signal that was missed and why it was decisive.

* For correct verdicts: which signal was the strongest, and which signals were ambiguous.

* The Scroller's Description Accuracy Score for this scenario (see Section 6).

| The Instant Reveal Is the Primary Learning Moment Players do not learn by getting things right. They learn by understanding exactly what they missed and why — immediately after they missed it. The Instant Reveal is the pedagogical core of KONAN, not the gameplay that precedes it. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 5  The Three-Tier Verdict System**

KONAN uses a three-tier verdict system rather than binary true/false sorting. This reflects the reality that most real-world misinformation is not purely fabricated — it is true content applied to a false context, legitimate sources making misleading claims, or accurate information stripped of critical caveats.

Understanding the difference between Misleading and Fabricated is one of the most important skills the game develops.

| Verdict | Definition | Real-World Examples |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **VERIFIED** | The content is accurate, the source is credible, and the claim is supported by corroborating evidence from at least two independent verified sources. | A typhoon alert issued by PAGASA with cross-corroboration from three major news outlets. All metadata, account history, and domain authority checks pass. |
| **MISLEADING** | Some or all content is factually accurate, but the framing, context, timing, or headline creates a false impression. The intent to deceive may be present, but the core claim is not entirely fabricated. | A genuine photo of crop damage — but taken during a different typhoon five years ago, recaptioned as current. The photo is real; the context is false. Swipe: Report. |
| **FABRICATED** | The content is entirely invented, AI-generated, or deliberately falsified. No credible source corroborates the core claim, and verification tools reveal active signs of manipulation or deception. | An AI-generated drone photo paired with a false headline from a 48-hour-old bot account with artificially inflated followers and no corroborating coverage anywhere. |

**Choosing Between Misleading and Fabricated**

The most common error new players make is classifying Misleading content as Fabricated. This distinction matters — in real life, treating a misleading source as entirely fabricated can cause players to dismiss legitimate journalism that has been shared in a misleading context.

Use this decision rule:

| If you can answer YES to this question: | Verdict |
| :---- | :---- |
| Is the core image, video, or primary source element authentic and verifiable? | **MISLEADING — if the framing is false** |
| Are all primary elements of the post invented, AI-generated, or unverifiable? | **FABRICATED** |
| Does the source have a documented history and is the specific claim corroborated? | **VERIFIED — even if the topic is unfamiliar** |

**The Confidence Slider**

Every verdict must be accompanied by a Confidence Level from 1 to 5\. This is not cosmetic — it directly affects scoring.

| Level | Meaning | Scoring Effect |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **1** | Very uncertain — strong educated guess | No bonus or penalty for being wrong. |
| **2** | Somewhat uncertain — leaning toward this verdict | 5% bonus if correct; no penalty if wrong. |
| **3** | Moderately confident — reasonable evidence base | 10% bonus if correct; 5% penalty if wrong. |
| **4** | Confident — strong multi-signal evidence | 20% bonus if correct; 15% penalty if wrong. |
| **5** | Certain — all signals align, full process completed | 35% bonus if correct; 30% penalty if wrong. |

The Confidence Slider teaches epistemic humility. Players who develop the habit of high confidence on insufficient evidence will be penalized not for being wrong — but for being overconfident. This mirrors the real risk of misinformation: the most dangerous sharers are those who are certain they are right.

**SECTION 6  Scoring System**

KONAN's scoring system rewards deliberation, process, and accuracy — in that order. Speed is not scored. The fastest verdict earns no more than a thorough one.

**Base Score Per Scenario**

| Action / Outcome | Scroller | Verifier |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Correct verdict, full process completed (2+ tools, all checklist items) | \+100 pts | \+100 pts |
| Correct verdict, incomplete process (1 tool only or checklist gaps) | \+60 pts | \+40 pts |
| Incorrect verdict, full process completed | \+20 pts | \+20 pts |
| Incorrect verdict, incomplete process | 0 pts | 0 pts |
| Unsupported verdict (less than 2 tool checks) | Score × 0.75 | Score × 0.75 |

**Confidence Modifier**

Apply the Confidence Modifier from Section 5 on top of the base score, after all other modifiers. The Confidence Modifier applies to both players equally, since both agreed on (or failed to challenge) the confidence level before the verdict was executed.

**Description Accuracy Score (Scroller Only)**

After each Instant Reveal, the Verifier rates the Scroller's description quality across four dimensions. This score does not affect the shared scenario score — it is a personal development metric for the Scroller, tracked across the session and surfaced in the Round Debrief.

| Dimension | Rating (1–5) | What It Measures |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Completeness** | 1 (missed items) – 5 (all five covered) | Did the description cover all five checklist items unprompted? |
| **Precision** | 1 (vague) – 5 (exact) | Were account names, claims, and visual details described specifically enough to be actionable? |
| **Neutrality** | 1 (opinionated) – 5 (neutral) | Did the Scroller describe what they saw without leading the Verifier toward a predetermined conclusion? |
| **Anomaly Awareness** | 1 (nothing noted) – 5 (all anomalies flagged) | Did the Scroller proactively flag visual or textual anomalies before being asked? |

The Description Accuracy Score is the primary feedback mechanism for developing the observational communication skills that transfer directly to real-world MIL practice.

**Role Mastery Badge**

A Role Mastery Badge is awarded to a player only when all of the following conditions are met across a single session:

* Completed both the Scroller and Verifier roles (Rounds 1 and 2).

* Achieved a Description Accuracy Score average of 3.5 or above across the round played as Scroller.

* Completed at least 2 tool checks in every scenario played as Verifier.

* Completed Round 3 solo assessment.

The Role Mastery Badge is a prerequisite for participation in any train-the-trainer program, where badge holders become KONAN session facilitators for new players.

**SECTION 7  The Debrief Protocol**

The Debrief Protocol is mandatory and structured. It is not a review of the score. It is the phase where the deepest learning from each session occurs — where instincts developed during play are examined, challenged, and refined.

There are three levels of debrief in a KONAN session. Each serves a distinct purpose.

**Level 1 — Instant Reveal (After Every Scenario)**

Described in Section 4, Phase 6\. Duration: 2–3 minutes. Conducted by the game system automatically. Both players review the Instant Reveal screen together before proceeding to the next scenario. No player may skip the Instant Reveal or advance before the other has finished reading it.

**Level 2 — Round Summary (After Every 5 Scenarios)**

At the end of each round, a Round Summary screen displays aggregated patterns from the five scenarios just completed:

* Which scenario type (Verified / Misleading / Fabricated) had the highest error rate.

* Which verification tool was most decisive and which was most underused.

* The Scroller's average Description Accuracy Score for the round.

* The most common error: incorrect verdict, unsupported verdict, or missed anomaly.

Players discuss the Round Summary for 5 minutes before switching roles or proceeding to the next round. The facilitator (if present) uses the Facilitator Prompt Card specific to the patterns the Round Summary reveals.

**Level 3 — Session Debrief (End of Full Session)**

The Session Debrief is a 15-minute facilitated discussion conducted after Round 3\. It is the most important phase of the entire session. Three structured questions anchor the discussion:

| Q1 | Which post in this session would you be most likely to share on your real feed — and why? This question is asked before the verdict is revealed, to probe honest instinct rather than post-hoc rationalization. |
| :---: | :---- |
| **Q2** | **What was the hardest thing about being the Verifier? About being the Scroller?** Designed to surface the specific friction points in each role and connect them to real-world experience. |
| **Q3** | **If you saw a post like \[the highest-error scenario from the session\] on your real feed tomorrow, what is the first thing you would do differently now?** A behavioral commitment question, not a knowledge question. The answer is a personal MIL action plan. |

The Facilitator Debrief Card for each session is generated from the Round Summary data and contains the specific scenario references, error patterns, and follow-up prompts tailored to what actually happened in that group's session.

**SECTION 8  The Scenario System — How Content Is Built**

KONAN scenarios are not static. They are generated from a modular component system that produces thousands of unique combinations, preventing memorization and keeping the verification process active on every playthrough.

**The Five Scenario Modules**

Each scenario is assembled from one element of each of the five modules below. These elements are combined at runtime, meaning no two sessions need to present the same scenario configuration.

| Module | Options | What It Adds to the Scenario |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Account Profile** | New account (0–7 days) / Established account (1–5 years) / Hijacked account (old account, recent behavior change) | Determines what account history tools will reveal. |
| **Content Type** | Static image / Video clip / Text headline / Composite (image \+ caption mismatch) | Determines which verification tools are most relevant. |
| **Claim Category** | Health & Science / Finance & Markets / Politics & Elections / Environment & Climate / Community & Local Events | Determines which cross-source corroboration databases are queried. |
| **Fabrication Type** | AI-generated visual / Outdated content re-contextualized / Edited authentic content / Entirely invented narrative / Authentic content (Verified) | Determines the correct verdict tier and the primary verification signal. |
| **Deception Sophistication** | Novice (obvious tells) / Intermediate (requires 2+ tool checks) / Advanced (all signals ambiguous until full process) | Sets scenario difficulty and point value. |

**Process-Based Verification — What the Game Teaches**

KONAN does not teach players to spot AI content by looking for visual artifacts such as wrong shadows, extra fingers, or inconsistent lighting. These tells are real today — but AI generation is eliminating them rapidly. A player who learns to verify only by spotting artifacts will be unprepared for content that has no visible tells.

KONAN teaches players to run a process regardless of how convincing the content looks. The process is the skill that transfers to any generation of AI content, any type of disinformation, and any platform.

| Do NOT teach (Artifact-Based) | DO teach (Process-Based) |
| :---- | :---- |
| "The shadow is pointing the wrong way — it must be AI." "The hands look wrong." "The text in the image has weird letters." "It looks too perfect to be real." | "The account was created 48 hours ago — check posting frequency and follower growth rate." "No corroborating source covers this event — run cross-source check." "The WHOIS shows domain registered yesterday — flag for domain authority check." "Image metadata shows editing software signature — check original source." |

| The Temporal Signal Rule Account history, posting patterns, domain registration dates, and cross-source coverage are temporal signals — they involve time-based evidence that AI cannot fake without establishing a genuine digital footprint over time. These signals are the most durable verification tools available and form the core of KONAN's curriculum. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 9  Assessment Framework — Measuring Real-World Transfer**

KONAN's in-game score measures performance within the simulation. The Assessment Framework measures what matters more: whether players make different decisions in real life after playing.

The framework has three stages. All three must be administered for any session that is part of a formal MIL program, school curriculum, or UNESCO evaluation.

**Stage 1 — Pre-Assessment (Before Play, 5 Minutes)**

Administer before players receive any game instructions. Do not tell players they are being assessed — frame it as an icebreaker.

Players are shown 5 social media posts (drawn from the assessment bank, separate from the gameplay scenario bank). They make one decision per post: Would you share this? Yes / No / Unsure. No explanation is required. Their responses are recorded anonymously and saved for Stage 3 comparison.

| Why It Matters The Pre-Assessment captures baseline sharing behavior before any MIL intervention. Without this baseline, there is no way to know whether KONAN changed anything. |
| :---- |

**Stage 2 — Post-Session Debrief (After Play, 10–15 Minutes)**

The facilitated discussion from Section 7, Level 3\. This stage is both a learning consolidation tool and an interim qualitative assessment. The three structured questions surface behavioral intentions and emerging instincts.

Facilitators record key responses from the Session Debrief — not to evaluate players, but to identify which scenarios and which misconceptions most commonly appear in this group. This data informs follow-up sessions and curriculum adjustments.

**Stage 3 — Post-Assessment (One Week Later, 5 Minutes)**

One week after the session, players are presented with the same 5 posts from the Pre-Assessment under the same conditions: Would you share this? Yes / No / Unsure.

The behavioral change between Stage 1 and Stage 3 is the actual learning outcome metric. Report this as:

* Share rate change: the percentage of posts players chose to share, pre vs. post.

* Hesitation rate change: the percentage of Unsure responses, pre vs. post.

* Refusal rate change: the percentage of No responses, pre vs. post.

A successful KONAN intervention shows a meaningful increase in refusal rate and hesitation rate for the posts that the game's curriculum specifically addressed — not just an overall attitude shift.

| For UNESCO Evaluation The Assessment Framework transforms KONAN from a game with a score into a learning intervention with evidence. Stage 1 and Stage 3 data are the primary metrics for any formal evaluation of KONAN's impact. Facilitators should report this data in aggregate, anonymized form to the KONAN team for program-level analysis. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 10  Accessibility Modes**

KONAN is designed to be playable by the broadest possible range of participants. Three accessibility modes modify the core experience to accommodate different needs. Facilitators select the appropriate mode before the session begins.

| Mode | Who It Serves | Key Modifications |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Standard** | Players without accessibility requirements. Default mode. | Real-time verbal communication. Swipe actions. Standard feed speed. |
| **Text Relay Mode** | Players who are deaf, hard of hearing, mute, or playing online with poor audio quality. | All communication between Scroller and Verifier is typed rather than spoken. The Scroller types into a shared text channel visible to the Verifier. Time limits are extended by 60 seconds per scenario to account for typing speed. |
| **Extended Mode** | Players with visual processing differences (dyslexia, low vision), cognitive processing differences, or motor impairments. | Feed speed reduced by 50% with no scoring penalty. Dyslexia-friendly font applied throughout. Swipe actions replaced by large, clearly labeled Share and Report buttons with keyboard navigation. No time penalties for returning to a post. |

All three modes cover the same scenarios and produce the same learning outcomes. Score comparisons between sessions in different modes are not meaningful and should not be made.

| Facilitator Responsibility Accessibility mode selection is the facilitator's responsibility, not the players'. Facilitators must ask about accessibility needs before each session and select the appropriate mode before play begins. Discovering mid-session that a player needs Extended Mode is disruptive and avoidable. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 11  Deployment Tiers**

KONAN is deployable in three tiers to accommodate the full range of infrastructure environments in which MIL programming occurs — from well-resourced schools to community centers without reliable internet access.

**Tier 1 — Full Cooperative (Standard)**

* Requirements: One mobile device (Scroller) \+ one desktop or laptop (Verifier) per pair. Stable internet connection.

* Session capacity: Up to 15 pairs (30 players) simultaneously.

* Features: Full real-time cooperative experience. All verification tools active. All accessibility modes available. Assessment Framework fully automated.

* Recommended for: Schools with computer labs, universities, youth organization offices, and hackathon venues.

**Tier 2 — Single-Device Mode**

* Requirements: One device per pair (mobile, tablet, or desktop). Internet connection recommended but not required for cached sessions.

* Session capacity: Unlimited (no server-side synchronization required).

* Features: Turn-based mode. The Scroller phase is timed (30 seconds of viewing, no pausing). The Verifier phase follows immediately on the same device. Role rotation is still mandatory. All three rounds still apply.

* Limitation: Real-time communication between players is replaced by structured note-taking: the Scroller writes their description on a physical notepad before passing the device. This preserves the communication separation while working around the single-device constraint.

* Recommended for: Schools with limited device access, community centers, and any setting where pairing devices is impractical.

**Tier 3 — Offline Card Mode**

* Requirements: Printed Scenario Deck \+ Verification Packet. No device, no internet.

* Session capacity: Unlimited.

* Features: Scenario cards contain the post content (printed image, caption, account details). Verification Packets contain the metadata, domain information, account history, and corroboration data that the Verifier would normally access via dashboard tools. All rules, roles, verdict system, and debrief protocols remain identical.

* Limitation: Scenario pool is fixed per printed deck. Procedural generation is not available. Decks must be reprinted to add new scenarios.

* Recommended for: Rural schools, barangay community events, areas with no electricity or connectivity, and large-group workshops where device distribution is impractical.

| Choosing the Right Tier Select the lowest-friction tier for your specific context. A Tier 3 session with full engagement produces better MIL outcomes than a Tier 1 session disrupted by connectivity problems, device shortages, or technical difficulties. Tier 3 is not a lesser experience — it is KONAN adapted for equity. |
| :---- |

**SECTION 12  Facilitator Guide**

The facilitator's role is not to play KONAN — it is to ensure that the debrief is as rich as the gameplay. A well-run debrief can double the learning impact of any session.

**Before the Session**

4. Confirm accessibility needs and select the appropriate mode (Section 10).

5. Select the deployment tier appropriate for your venue (Section 11).

6. Administer the Pre-Assessment (Stage 1, Section 9\) before explaining any game rules.

7. Brief players on roles and the Description Checklist (Section 4). Do not explain the Verdict System in detail — let players discover the Misleading tier through play.

8. Set the room expectation: this is not a competition. There is no leaderboard shared between pairs. The goal is learning, not winning.

**During the Session**

9. Do not intervene in gameplay unless a technical issue arises or a player is in genuine distress.

10. Observe the Scroller's description quality. Note specific phrases or omissions that you can reference in the debrief.

11. After each Round Summary, prompt the pair with one facilitator question before they switch roles:

    * After Round 1: "What did the Verifier wish the Scroller had told them?"

    * After Round 2: "Was anything harder about this role than you expected?"

    * After Round 3: "What did you do differently when you had to do both roles yourself?"

12. Allow the Instant Reveal to run without commentary. Let players read and react before you speak.

**Running the Session Debrief**

The three Session Debrief questions from Section 7 are your primary tools. Use this sequencing:

13. Open with Q1 before revealing any final scores. The goal is honest instinct, not performance.

14. Use Q2 to surface frustration — frustration is productive. If players found the Scroller role difficult, ask why. The answer will connect to their real feed behavior.

15. Close with Q3 as a behavioral commitment. Ask players to say their answer aloud or write it on a card they keep. A stated behavioral commitment is more likely to transfer than an internal one.

**After the Session**

16. Record the Round Summary data for your group (highest-error scenario type, most missed tool, average Description Accuracy Score).

17. Schedule the Post-Assessment (Stage 3, Section 9\) for exactly one week later. Send a calendar reminder or physical note on the day.

18. Identify any players who earned or are close to a Role Mastery Badge. These players are candidates for the train-the-trainer program.

19. Submit aggregated, anonymized Stage 1 and Stage 3 data to the KONAN program team for impact analysis.
